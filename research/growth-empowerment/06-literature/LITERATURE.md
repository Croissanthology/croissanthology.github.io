# Is this already part of economics?

Short answer: yes, mostly under other names. Political economy has long asked why rulers treat subjects well or badly, and its answers turn on what Margot is circling: who the ruler needs, whether people can leave, and whether the pie is growing. There is no single "disempowerment curve", but several measured series come close (section 9).

**Sourcing.** Every claim comes from a source looked up in this session (November 2026). Some hosts could not be opened from this machine (intelligence-curse.ai, arxiv.org, MIT economics, proceedings.mlr.press). For those I relied on search-engine excerpts and bibliographic records, not on reading the full text. Details resting only on secondary summaries are flagged.

## 1. Stationary bandits and "encompassing interest"

**Idea.** A bandit who settles down does better by taxing moderately and protecting his victims than by looting them. The larger the share of output a ruler keeps (his "encompassing interest"), the more he wants output to grow.

**Sources.** Olson, *APSR* 1993 ([PDF](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Olson%20-%201993%20-%20Dictatorship,%20Democracy,%20and%20Development.pdf)); McGuire and Olson, *JEL* 1996 ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jeclit/v34y1996i1p72-96.html)).

**What it says.** A secure autocrat keeps order and provides public goods; an insecure one with a short horizon confiscates. Succession uncertainty limits autocracies over the long run. McGuire and Olson show that a ruling majority that also earns market income redistributes less than an autocrat, because it bears more of the cost of distortion.

**Bearing.** This is the cleanest existing model of a "soft wind of incentives". But the active ingredients are the ruler's time horizon and his dependence on people's productive effort, not growth as such.

## 2. Serfdom, the Black Death, and outside options

**Idea.** Domar (1970): of free land, free peasants and non-working landlords, you can have any two but not all three. Where labor is scarce it is valuable, so elites either pay it or tie it down.

**Sources.** Domar ([MIT copy](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/63299/causesofslaveryh00doma.pdf)); the Brenner debate ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/brenner-debate/introduction/DDAF14CA1FFD448D9ABB750A722860AF); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brenner_debate)); Acemoglu and Wolitzky, *Econometrica* 2011 ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15581)).

**Evidence.** After the Black Death, serfdom faded in the west and intensified east of the Elbe. Postan and Hatcher stressed population; Brenner argued that class power decided the outcome: the same labor shock helped peasants where their communities were strong and lords where they were weak. Peters ([2019](https://becklab.hms.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/pegroup/files/peters2019.pdf)) treats eastern re-enserfment as a sovereign's political choice. Cross-country tests find no definite serfdom/land-labor-ratio relation ([Klein](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/centres/cage/manage/news/klein_web_aut_18.pdf)). Acemoglu and Wolitzky formalize this: better outside options mean less coercion, but labor scarcity has an *ambiguous* effect. It raises coercion if it raises the value of output more than it improves workers' alternatives.

**Bearing.** This is the sharpest answer to "serfs were necessary but not empowered". Being needed makes the powerful want to *keep* you, and keeping can mean paying or chaining. Outside options and the capacity to organize decide which. Current LLMs fit the serf case: necessary, no exit, no collective voice.

## 3. Selectorate theory

**Idea.** Leaders need a "winning coalition" drawn from a larger "selectorate". Small coalitions are cheapest to buy with private rewards; large ones with public goods.

**Source.** Bueno de Mesquita, Smith, Siverson and Morrow, *The Logic of Political Survival* (2003) ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selectorate_theory)). Critiques: [Clarke and Stone](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psc/stone/articles/clarkestone2008.pdf) say the democracy effect is mis-estimated; [Hanson and Gallagher](https://websites.umich.edu/~jkhanson/resources/HansonGallagherMPSA2012.pdf) say selectorate size barely matters.

**Bearing.** A precise version of "the empowerment is in the plurality". Sally is not pivotal; she belongs to a coalition so large that public goods are the cheapest bribe. The AI worry restated: if the coalition can become compute plus a few people, public goods stop being the efficient payment.

## 4. Resource curse, Intelligence Curse, Gradual Disempowerment

**Idea.** States funded by oil instead of taxpayers need citizens less and can buy them off or repress them.

**Evidence.** Ross, *World Politics* 2001 ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/abs/does-oil-hinder-democracy/67665D8D240C8F43CD4A2DCB35894071)), finds at least limited support for rentier, repression and modernization channels. Haber and Menaldo, *APSR* 2011 ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/do-natural-resources-fuel-authoritarianism-a-reappraisal-of-the-resource-curse/0DC2DEF9747F89CA6894A6245E80B6F7)), use within-country histories and find no link, sometimes a "resource blessing". Ross's 2015 survey ([Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-polisci-052213-040359)) concludes there is "considerable evidence" of a political curse from *petroleum* under certain conditions. He notes that a significant minority think it a statistical artifact, and that some effects appear only after the 1970s. Honest summary: real but conditional and contested.

**The Intelligence Curse** (Drago and Laine, April 2025; [site](https://intelligence-curse.ai/), [series summary](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/GbZp5xyHs34e9RCyt/the-intelligence-curse-an-essay-series)). Once AGI replaces human labor in economically and militarily important roles, states and firms lose the incentive to invest in ordinary people. It is explicitly modeled on the rentier state. The "Breaking" chapter ([link](https://intelligence-curse.ai/breaking/)) proposes three things: avert catastrophes without centralizing control; diffuse AI that augments rather than automates people; and democratize institutions, which alone "won't be stable". (Read via excerpts; the site was unreachable from here.)

**Gradual Disempowerment** (Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger, Duvenaud; [arXiv 2501.16946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946); ICML 2025 position paper). Incremental AI can erode both explicit human control (voting, consumer choice) and the *implicit* alignment of economy, culture and states with human interests. That implicit alignment exists because those systems depend on human participation. The losses reinforce each other across systems, and the authors say no one has a concrete plan to stop this.

**Bearing.** Margot's neighbors already treat her "soft wind" as the thing at risk; "implicit alignment" is close to a formal name for it. The Haber–Menaldo dispute warns that the oil analogy is weaker evidence than it sounds.

## 5. Taxes, mobile capital, and representation

- **Tilly, *Coercion, Capital, and European States*** (1990) ([overview](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Coercion,_Capital,_and_European_States,_AD_990%E2%80%931992)). Coercion-intensive states (Russia) squeezed peasants via landlords. Capital-intensive ones (Venice) bargained with merchants. Mixed "capitalized coercion" (England, France) won.
- **Bates and Lien** (1985) ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/003232928501400102)). The more mobile a citizen's assets, the better their bargain with a revenue-seeking state.
- **North and Weingast** (1989). Parliament's post-1688 power made royal promises credible and borrowing cheap. Sussman and Yafeh ([CEPR](https://cepr.org/publications/dp4404)) find British interest rates stayed high and politically sensitive for decades. [Pincus and Robinson](https://people.bu.edu/chamley/HSFref/PincusRobinson11.pdf) defend 1688 as a turning point for other reasons.
- **North, Wallis and Weingast, *Violence and Social Orders*** (2009) ([chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/violence-and-social-orders/transition-from-limited-to-open-access-orders-the-doorstep-conditions/2994147E12F110E07F11C6EC9F3E401D)). "Limited access orders" keep peace by sharing rents among elites. "Open access orders" let anyone form organizations, and that competition sustains both growth and democracy.
- **Acemoglu and Robinson, *QJE* 2000** ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:115:y:2000:i:4:p:1167-1199.)). Elites extended the vote as a credible promise under threat of revolution. Aidt and Jensen find 1820–1938 European data consistent with this ([repo](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/241860)).
- ***The Narrow Corridor*** (2019) ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2019/narrow-corridor-acemoglu-liberty-0924)). Liberty survives only when state and society grow stronger together (the "Red Queen" race). China is classed as a "despotic Leviathan".

**Bearing.** Sally's power comes from belonging to a mobile, taxable, organizable mass whose revenue and quiescence the state needs. In these terms the AI threat is that revenue arrives without bargaining with citizens, and the revolt threat dies once enforcement needs no people.

## 6. Does growth cause democracy, or the reverse?

- **Lipset (1959)** launched modernization theory.
- **Przeworski and Limongi (1997)** ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/24CC3E289332FF2D39B5FACEAD75C408)): development does not trigger democracy, but democracies survive in rich countries.
- **Boix and Stokes (2003)** ([PDF](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2020/POLn4002/um/Boix_and_Stokes.pdf?kod=SPp411)): restore an endogenous effect using pre-1950 data.
- **Acemoglu, Johnson, Robinson and Yared (2008)** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11205)): with country fixed effects, the income–democracy link vanishes.
- **Treisman (2015)** ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v59y2015i4p927-942.html)): income matters mainly after a leader leaves, and over 10–20 years rather than 1–5.
- **Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo and Robinson (2019)** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20004)): democratization raises GDP per capita by about 20% in the long run.

**Honest state.** Democracy causing growth is fairly well supported. Growth causing democracy is contested: weak in the short run, visible in the medium run and around leadership changes. The best-supported reading is that wealth helps democracies *survive* far more than it *creates* them. Margot's own caveat ("when you don't begin from complete autocracy") fits this well.

## 7. Growth, stagnation, zero-sum thinking

- **Benjamin Friedman, *The Moral Consequences of Economic Growth*** (2005) ([review](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2006/01/growth-is-good.html)). Broad rising living standards foster tolerance, mobility and democracy; stagnation, even without collapse, endangers them. The evidence is historical narrative; reviewers' main counterexample is the Great Depression, which did not make America less democratic.
- **Chinoy, Nunn, Sequeira and Stantcheva** ([NBER w31688](https://nber.org/papers/w31688); [AEA summary](https://aeaweb.org/research/zero-sum-politics-us)). In a survey of about 20,000 Americans, people whose families saw more upward mobility are less zero-sum. Zero-sum views predict support for redistribution and for immigration restriction, and younger cohorts are more zero-sum.
- **Funke, Schularick and Trebesch** ([VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/political-aftermath-financial-crises-going-extremes)). Across 1870–2014, the far-right vote rises about 30% after *financial* crises. Ordinary recessions do not do this, and the effect fades within about a decade.

**Bearing.** Margot's "regular return on investment" psychology has direct survey support. But politics reacts more to shocks and perceived unfairness than to the growth rate itself.

## 8. China, and North Korea

- **Performance legitimacy.** Zhao Dingxin ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0002764209338800)) calls it "inherently unstable", a likely source of crisis if growth slows.
- **Promotion tournaments.** Li and Zhou 2005 ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v89y2005i9-10p1743-1762.html)): provincial leaders with better growth were more likely to be promoted. Shih, Adolph and Liu 2012 ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/B22B6ACD187AD664CCCD6497E6A165BE)): factional ties, education and revenue collection, not growth, predict advancement. One article reports that correcting miscoded data erases Li and Zhou's result ([Luo and Qin 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11366-021-09739-2.pdf)); I did not check the replication itself.
- **Decentralization.** Xu Chenggang 2011 ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:jeclit:v:49:y:2011:i:4:p:1076-1151)) calls the system "regionally decentralized authoritarianism": central control of personnel, local control of the economy. Montinola, Qian and Weingast 1995 ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/world-politics/article/abs/federalism-chinese-style-the-political-basis-for-economic-success-in-china/6F839D520DAE1BCB2721B36BA5E34396)) argue that "federalism, Chinese style" protected markets, while noting it was not institutionalized.
- **Measured outcomes.**
  - Freedom House 2025: China 9/100, North Korea 3/100 ([China](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2129026.html), [NK](https://freedomhouse.org/country/north-korea/freedom-world/2025)).
  - About 800 million Chinese left extreme poverty in 40 years ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/04/01/lifting-800-million-people-out-of-poverty-new-report-looks-at-lessons-from-china-s-experience)). Life expectancy is about 78 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/SPDYNLE00INCHN)).
  - Satisfaction with government rose almost everywhere in 2003–2016 surveys; the authors warn satisfaction is not legitimacy ([Ash Center](https://ash.harvard.edu/files/ash/files/final_policy_brief_7.6.2020.pdf)).
  - The same party presided over the Great Leap famine. Most demographic estimates put it at roughly 23–30 million excess deaths, with a range of about 15–45 million ([Meng, Qian, Yared](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/qian/resources/MPQY_FINAL.pdf); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1127087)).

**Bearing.** Chinese citizens are *materially* much better off and largely satisfied, but near the bottom on control over their own outcomes. "Broadly empowered" holds for welfare, not for empowerment as the brief defines it. Their gains come from a ruler's interest and incentive design that citizens cannot enforce. North Korea is the same structure with a different ruler's choice.

## 9. Exit, voice, bargaining power, and candidate curves

**Exit and voice.** Hirschman (1970): members of a declining organization can leave or complain, and loyalty delays leaving ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Exit,_Voice,_and_Loyalty)). Tiebout (1956): mobile residents choosing among many jurisdictions discipline governments ([overview](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_voting)). Sally's power is mostly exit, which works only while there are many employers and places to exit to.

**Monopsony.** Employer concentration is associated with lower posted wages ([Azar, Marinescu, Steinbaum](https://docs.iza.org/dp11254.pdf)). Berger, Herkenhoff and Mongey estimate 7.6% welfare losses from labor market power ([via RBA review](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2023/2023-02/literature.html)). Evidence on whether concentration is rising is mixed.

**Candidate "disempowerment curves".**
- **Labor share.** US nonfarm labor share: about 66% in 1947, about 63% in 2000, 52.8% in Q2 2026, a record low ([BLS 2017](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2017/labor-share-of-output-has-declined-since-1947.htm); [BLS 2026](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/labor-share-at-its-lowest-level-52-8-percent-in-second-quarter-2026.htm)). Explanations are disputed:
  - cheaper capital goods ([Karabarbounis and Neiman](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19136.pdf)), challenged by the [Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/swp2019-3.pdf);
  - a shift toward "superstar" firms ([Autor et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/W23396));
  - measurement ([Heritage](https://www.heritage.org/node/10610/print-display)).
- **Union density.** OECD: halved from 30% (1985) to 15% (2023/24) ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/membership-of-unions-and-employers-organisations-and-bargaining-coverage_fe47107c-en/full-report.html)). US: 20.1% (1983) to 9.9% (2024), BLS data via [Statista](https://statista.com/chart/9919/the-state-of-the-unions).
- **V-Dem.** Democracy for the average world citizen is back to 1985 levels (2025 report) and 1978 levels (2026 report) ([2026](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf)).
- **State capacity versus tax base.** Besley and Persson model fiscal capacity as an investment encouraged by common goals and inclusive institutions ([PDF](https://www.lse.ac.uk/economics/Assets/Documents/personal-pages/tim-besley/selected-publications/the-origins-of-state-capacity.pdf)). I found no clean series linking tax-base composition to citizen power.
- **Automation.** Acemoglu and Restrepo: automation always lowers the labor share unless new human tasks are created to reinstate labor ([JEP](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.33.2.3)). Korinek and Suh simulate AGI scenarios in which wages collapse toward the return on capital before automation is complete ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255)).

## 10. Formal notions of power

- **Power indices** measure *necessity to decisions*, exactly what Sally lacks:
  - Shapley–Shubik (1954): how often a voter is pivotal ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley%E2%80%93Shubik_power_index));
  - Banzhaf (1965): a similar index, used to show Nassau County's weighted voting was unfair ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banzhaf_power_index)).
- **Empowerment as channel capacity.** Klyubin, Polani and Nehaniv (2005) define empowerment as the maximum information an agent's actions can inject into its own future ([PDF](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/282/1/901241.pdf)). This is the best formal match for "how many futures you can reach", and it is independent of necessity.
- **Capabilities.** Sen: what people are effectively able to do and be, not what they own ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)).
- **Power-seeking.** Turner et al. (NeurIPS 2021): for most goals, optimal agents tend to keep options open and seek power ([proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/c26820b8a4c1b3c2aa868d6d57e14a79-Abstract.html)).

"Mathematically defensible" therefore means: pick one of these definitions and show it moves as the essay claims.

## 11. Wealth condensation

In the yard-sale model, agents trade at random and each trade puts a share of the poorer party's wealth at stake. Even fair coin flips concentrate nearly all wealth in one "oligarch" ([Boghosian, *SciAm* 2019](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-inequality-inevitable/)). Adding a bias favoring the richer trader gives a phase transition to oligarchy ([Boghosian et al., Physica A 2017](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:phsmap:v:476:y:2017:i:c:p:15-37)). Bouchaud and Mézard found a similar condensation ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0002374.pdf)). Boghosian: free markets "cannot be stable without redistribution mechanisms" ([Tufts](https://now.tufts.edu/2017/10/12/mathematics-inequality)). This is a toy model of "zero-sum produces a big baddie", and a warning that adding growth may not help unless gains are spread out.

## What economics already knows that answers Margot

Necessity alone does not protect people: a ruler who needs your labor may pay you or coerce you, and your outside option decides which. Rulers treat people well when their own income depends on those people's willing effort and they expect to stay in power. Large coalitions get public goods and small coalitions get private rewards, so "empowerment in the plurality" is a known result. States that need taxes from mobile people must bargain with them; states with revenue from elsewhere (oil, perhaps AI) need not. Democracy fairly reliably raises growth, while growth mostly helps democracies survive rather than creating them. People who grew up with growth are less zero-sum, and financial crashes reliably feed extremism. The labor share, union density and democracy indices are all falling, and these are the closest measured disempowerment curves.

## Where the literature disagrees with Margot's hunch

- **Growth is not the active ingredient; bargaining position is.** In Olson, Tilly, Bates–Lien and Acemoglu–Robinson, growth helps only because rulers then depend on producers who can withhold effort, leave or revolt. Without those threats, growth enriches rulers: oil states grow.
- **Steady growth did not stop the measured slide.** The US labor share and union density fell through decades of growth, and V-Dem's average-citizen democracy score fell back to 1978 levels while the world economy grew.
- **The Chinese are not "broadly empowered".** They are materially far better off and report satisfaction, but score 9/100 on Freedom House. Their welfare rests on rulers' choices they cannot enforce, as the famine showed, and even the "promoted for growth" finding is contested.
- **Sally is not unnecessary.** Her individual power index is tiny, but her class's is large: revenue and stability depend on it. The literature puts her power at the class level, not in a "soft wind".
- **The causal arrow is weaker than she assumes.** Growth helps democracies survive but does little to create them.

## What seems genuinely open

- Can an empowerment measure kept *separate* from necessity (channel capacity, capabilities) be shown to rise with growth in a model, and which ingredient (exit, coalition size or growth itself) does the work?
- Do positive-sum *expectations* constrain rulers on their own, apart from material gains? The zero-sum findings hint at this; no one has tested it as a mechanism.
- What replaces the tax-and-revolt bargain when rulers need neither workers nor soldiers? The theories predict the problem but say little about which institutions would survive the transition.
- Is the falling labor share an early disempowerment curve or a measurement artifact?
- Why do necessary parties (serfs, LLMs) end up instrumentalized? A model that separates "being needed" from "being able to refuse" would be a real contribution.

## Reading list (no math background needed)

2. **Drago and Laine, *The Intelligence Curse*** ([site](https://intelligence-curse.ai/)). Your direct interlocutors, and the rentier state applied to AI.
3. **Kulveit et al., "Gradual Disempowerment"** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16946)). Names the "implicit alignment" Margot calls a soft wind.
4. **Acemoglu and Robinson, *The Narrow Corridor*** ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2019/narrow-corridor-acemoglu-liberty-0924)). Why state and society must grow stronger together, with China as the counterexample.
5. **AEA summary of the zero-sum paper** ([AEA](https://aeaweb.org/research/zero-sum-politics-us)). Short evidence that experiencing growth changes how people see winners and losers.
6. **Friedman, *The Moral Consequences of Economic Growth*** ([review](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2006/01/growth-is-good.html)). The book-length case for Margot's hunch; read it alongside its critics.
7. **Boghosian, "Is Inequality Inevitable?"** ([SciAm](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-inequality-inevitable/)). The zero-sum "big baddie" as a toy model, explained without equations.
8. **Ross, "What Have We Learned about the Resource Curse?"** ([PDF](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/jaro2023/POLn4002/um/Ross2015.pdf?lang=cs)). Shows how strong, and how contested, the oil analogy behind the AI worry is.
